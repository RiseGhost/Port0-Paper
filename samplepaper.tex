\begin{abstract}
This paper presents the design, development and exploratory evaluation of Port 0, a resource management video game in which the player operates a small data centre in a science-fiction setting. The game combines server hardware configuration, a typing-based command minigame and a periodic defence sequence that represents a DDoS attack, with the intention of exposing players to data centre terminology and concepts through gameplay. A single-group pre/post study was conducted remotely with 25 participants of mixed technical backgrounds, who played for about 19 minutes. Recognition increased for most terms that appear in the game, such as the ARM architecture, IBM as a CPU vendor, software installation through a terminal and DDoS attacks, whereas options not covered by the game, such as ransomware, remained unchanged. Given the small sample, the short exposure, the immediate post-test and the absence of a comparison group, these results are preliminary indications of term recognition and not evidence of management skills. Usability observations analysed with Nielsen's heuristics \cite{nielsen1994enhancing} and telemetry collected with Firebase informed interface changes. The results suggest that mechanics-driven simulation games are a promising way to introduce data centre vocabulary and justify controlled studies.

\keywords{Data centers simulation \and Management in data centers \and Simulation games \and Game-Based Learning.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% TODO: a comparação com cinema, TV e jornais foi retirada por falta de fonte. Se houver referência (e.g., Newzoo, PwC), pode ser reposta.
The video game industry has grown substantially over the past decade and, as it expands in scale and relevance, the application of video games increasingly extends beyond entertainment.

Serious games and simulation-based games provide players with interactive experiences that incorporate educational content. However, even games developed exclusively for entertainment require players to acquire new knowledge, develop complex skills and devise effective strategies in order to progress \cite{Martens2008}. Similarly, Miller \cite{Monica_Miller} suggests that the content of video games can facilitate indirect learning, that is, learning that occurs without the game being explicitly designed for educational purposes.

Motivated by these findings, this work investigates whether players acquire knowledge while interacting with \textbf{Port 0}, a resource management video game in which the player operates a small data centre within a fictional setting. Players must purchase and configure servers, fulfil client requests and defend the infrastructure against cyberattacks. Although the game is set in a science fiction universe, it incorporates simulation-based mechanics, including a word-based minigame and server configuration tasks.

We defined two levels of learning objectives (LO, i.e., the knowledge that players are expected to acquire by playing): \textbf{LO1}, recognition of terms related to server hardware, CPU architectures and vendors, operating systems, server software and cyberattacks; and \textbf{LO2}, conceptual understanding, e.g., that server components determine performance and energy consumption, that software can be installed through a terminal and that a DDoS attack disrupts service availability. This exploratory study addresses the following research questions (RQ, i.e., the questions that the evaluation seeks to answer):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1}: Does a short session of Port 0 change participants' recognition of the data centre terms embedded in its mechanics?
\item \textbf{RQ2}: Are such changes specific to content present in the game, when compared with content that the game does not address?
\item \textbf{RQ3}: Which usability problems hinder the first contact with the game?
\end{itemize}
The evaluation mainly measures LO1 and, partially, LO2.

\section{Related Work}

This section reviews the learning strategies that Port 0 draws on, related games about IT infrastructure and security, and the public interest in the game's theme.

\subsection{Learning through observation and through interaction}

Observational learning is the process by which beginners absorb rules, initial steps and complex procedures by watching experienced players, simulations or tutorials \cite{Martens2008}. When integrated into video games or simulations, it acts as a pedagogical ``scaffold'' for the learner \cite{Martens2008}. Titles such as CodeCombat \cite{codecombat}, Manufactoria \cite{manufactoria} and Labor Support Game \cite{labor_support_game} employ this strategy. In CodeCombat, for example, the code is highlighted line by line while the hero performs the corresponding action, so players directly observe the outcome of each command \cite{villareale2020}.

Observing a demonstration differs from learning through interaction, in which learners act on the system and receive feedback on the consequences of their actions \cite{villareale2020,Ibrahim2010}. Port 0 relies mainly on interaction and feedback, but its WordRush minigame also includes an observational component similar to CodeCombat: each completed command is shown in a history panel together with a short description of its effect (Section~\ref{Development of Port 0 game}).

\subsection{Trial and error and self-directed learning}

Trial and error is an active and exploratory strategy in which individuals test hypotheses in real time to determine which actions lead to success or failure \cite{villareale2020}. Learners repeatedly attempt tasks until they succeed or reach the highest score, understanding complex concepts through their own mistakes \cite{Marques2021,Sari2025}. Feedback loops reinforce this technique, as the system continuously responds to the player's actions \cite{Ibrahim2010}. Port 0 incorporates gameplay loops in which the player repeats a set of tasks and receives feedback in the form of payments, energy costs and attacks (Section~\ref{Design and development of Port 0 game}).

A related concept is self-directed learning, in which individuals take the initiative to diagnose their learning needs, choose resources and evaluate the outcomes \cite{knowles1975}. It is common in fields such as programming, where solutions are developed by continuously testing what works \cite{BaxterWebb2016}.

\subsection{Indirect learning}

Indirect learning is a process in which knowledge is acquired as a by-product of another activity, such as leisure, often without the individual realising that learning is taking place \cite{Lindberg2019,BaxterWebb2016}. Rather than explicitly teaching content, games create environments in which knowledge is constructed through experience, problem-solving and interaction with complex systems \cite{Monica_Miller}. Commercial games such as \textit{No Man's Sky} \cite{NoMansSky} and \textit{The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild} \cite{BOTW} promote experimentation, creativity, complex problem-solving and resource management; according to Miller, \textit{No Man's Sky} encourages behaviours similar to those used in scientific inquiry without explicitly teaching science \cite{Monica_Miller}.

Following this idea, Port 0 places technical terms in its environment, interface and tasks, so that players can encounter them while playing. In this paper, direct and indirect learning are treated as \emph{design intentions}: the evaluation does not distinguish between them.

\subsection{Games about IT infrastructure and security}

Several commercial games represent IT infrastructure. \textit{Data Center} \cite{datacenter} focuses on the physical layout of racks, power and cooling with high technical fidelity; \textit{PC Building Simulator} \cite{pcbs} reproduces the assembly of a computer and the installation of its operating system; \textit{Startup Company} \cite{startup} and \textit{Software Inc.} \cite{softinc} combine business management with server, power and cooling constraints; and \textit{Grey Hack} \cite{greyhack} simulates networks and hacking through command-line terminals. These titles are designed for entertainment and, to our knowledge, have not been evaluated as learning tools. In contrast, CyberCIEGE \cite{cyberciege} is a serious game in which players configure and protect a virtual network under budget constraints, and it has been used in information assurance education. More broadly, reviews of empirical studies associate games with knowledge acquisition and motivation, but also note that many studies rely on small samples and weak designs \cite{connolly2012}.

Port 0 lies between these groups: it simplifies the infrastructure represented in commercial simulators and adds fantasy elements, following design guidelines for educational games \cite{silva2020}, and it is evaluated, here in an exploratory way, for the recognition of the concepts it embeds.

\subsection{Public interest in the technology field}

Google Trends reports the relative search interest (0--100) for terms over time. Figure~\ref{fig:trends} shows the worldwide interest for the search terms \textit{technology} and \textit{data center} from August 2021 to August 2026 (monthly values, retrieved in August 2026). Interest in both terms remained roughly stable until mid-2025 and then rose sharply. Since the context of a game can raise a player's interest before play begins \cite{Martens2008} and the attractiveness of an educational game is related to its subject matter \cite{Marques2021}, we hypothesise that this growing public attention may increase learners' interest in the topic. This hypothesis is not tested in the present study.

\section{Design and development of Port 0 game}
\label{Design and development of Port 0 game}

\subsection{Design of Port 0 game}

Port 0 is set in a fantasy environment with science-fiction elements in which the player is an engineer working in a facility inspired by a data centre. The main tasks are to buy and configure servers, taking into account aspects such as processing capacity and energy consumption, and to complete client requests through a word-based minigame inspired by entering commands in a terminal. In addition, the player must defend the data centre from attacks using a cannon.

The game features two main gameplay loops. The task loop is the most common and longest-lasting: the player receives client requests and chooses whether to accept them; an accepted request is completed by playing the WordRush minigame. In the attack loop, the player uses a cannon to defend the data centre from hordes of enemies. Three game design techniques guided this concept: the MDA framework \cite{framework-mda}, Flow \cite{flow} and the Hook Model \cite{modelo-hook}. In MDA terms, the \emph{mechanics} are typing Linux commands in WordRush, configuring server hardware and operating the defence cannon; they give rise to \emph{dynamics} of resource management, balancing task revenue against energy expenses and adapting to attack interruptions; and the intended \emph{aesthetics} are a sense of technical mastery, with task monotony broken by action-oriented combat.

Flow \cite{flow} describes a balance between challenge and the player's abilities that avoids boredom and anxiety. It was used as a design reference: the attack loop was introduced to interrupt the potentially monotonous task loop and renew the challenge. Flow and engagement were design goals and were not measured in this study.

The Hook Model \cite{modelo-hook} describes a cycle of trigger, action, variable reward and investment intended to form habits. Because habit-forming design raises ethical concerns, particularly for young players, its use in Port 0 was limited to elements that sustain a play session: curiosity about the theme, short tasks of about one minute and variable payments from clients. The prototype has no monetisation, and planned reward elements such as trophies were not implemented. Client revenue continues to accrue while the game is closed, which acts as a return trigger; its effect on long-term play was not studied, as the evaluation consisted of a single supervised session.

\subsection{Development of Port 0 game}
\label{Development of Port 0 game}

Port 0 was developed with \textit{Unity} and \textit{C\#}, and the 3D models were created and textured in \textit{Blender}. The game is composed of several interconnected systems (Fig.~\ref{GameScene}).

The \textbf{shop system} allows the player to purchase a server through a sequence of steps in which components are configured, each with a short description (Fig.~\ref{fig:shop}). Every component has a price and an energy consumption in watts, and processors contribute to computing capacity in TFLOPS. Real units are used to connect the simulation with real hardware, although values are simplified. When the configured server has a high energy consumption, the shop displays a message recommending an ARM processor, reflecting the common positioning of ARM server processors as more energy-efficient. After the hardware, the player goes through a simulated operating system installation, in which the server is named.

The \textbf{economy system} periodically debits energy costs and credits one-off and recurring payments from clients; cooling, water and network costs were omitted in this version. The \textbf{task system} periodically presents client requests, each requiring server resources, which the player may accept or reject. The player earns money actively, by completing new tasks, and passively, through recurring payments from previously completed ones.

An accepted task is completed by playing \textbf{WordRush} (Fig.~\ref{WordRush}), which simulates an Ubuntu-styled terminal. The words of a command (e.g., \texttt{sudo}, \texttt{apt}, \texttt{install}, \texttt{mysqlserver}) move across the screen and the player must type them as displayed; players do not choose or compose the commands. Each completed command is added to a history panel together with a short description of its effect (e.g., ``Installs MySQL server''). Each task category corresponds to a server technology: Apache, MySQL, Node.js, Node.js with Nginx, and Postfix. WordRush therefore provides exposure to commands and their purpose, not practice in writing them.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{Images/WordRush_History.png}
    \caption{WordRush minigame: words of the current command move across the terminal (right); completed commands and their descriptions are listed in the history panel (left).}
    \label{WordRush}
\end{figure}

Finally, the \textbf{combat system} triggers an attack after a given number of completed tasks. The attack is announced by an e-mail and, while it lasts, recurring revenue is suspended but costs continue, until the player destroys all enemies with the cannon. The cannon is not intended to represent real DDoS mitigation, such as traffic filtering or rate limiting. It is a metaphor meant to convey that a data centre can be targeted and that an attack disrupts service availability and causes financial loss; its main design purpose is to break the monotony of the task loop.

\subsection{Procedure}

% TODO: confirmar o nome/referência da comissão de ética e como foi obtido o consentimento dos dois menores (e.g., encarregados de educação).
Sessions were conducted remotely through Discord or Google Meet, with participants sharing their screen. Each participant answered the pre-test questionnaire, played until the end of the first DDoS attack (on average 19 minutes, Table~\ref{Tab:TelemetricData}) and answered the post-test immediately afterwards. The researcher intervened only when a participant could not progress for more than two minutes. The study was approved by the ethics committee, participation was voluntary, the questionnaires were anonymous, and no personal data were collected nor profiles built.

\subsection{Instruments}

The pre-test included demographic questions (age, education, training in computer science and gaming habits) and nine knowledge items, which were repeated unchanged in the post-test. Six items were multiple-response questions, e.g., ``Which of the following are cyberattacks?'', mixing correct options, incorrect options and fictitious distractors; one was a yes/no question (``Can an individual create a Gmail-like e-mail service?''); and two asked which CPU vendors other than AMD and Intel, and which operating systems other than Windows and macOS, participants knew (open answers). For each option we report the number of participants who selected it before and after playing. Several options correspond to content that is not present in the game and act as a comparison for the effect of repeating the test. Because the two anonymous questionnaires were not linked at participant level, paired tests (e.g., exact McNemar) could not be computed, and the results are descriptive.
% TODO: confirmar que as respostas pré/pós não podem ser emparelhadas por participante.

For the usability analysis, the researcher observed each shared screen and recorded the problems encountered, which were afterwards classified according to Nielsen's heuristics \cite{nielsen1994enhancing}. Severity was assigned as \emph{low} (no direct impact on gameplay), \emph{medium} (reduced impact, without preventing progress) or \emph{high} (strong impact, may block progress).

Firebase recorded timestamps of gameplay events, from which we computed the time to purchase and configure the first server, the duration of the first attack (from its start until the last enemy is destroyed), the total time from the start of the session until the end of the first attack, and the time to complete each WordRush task. Six of the 25 telemetry records were discarded as invalid, due to game errors or external factors, leaving 19 records.

The largest increases occurred in options directly present in the game: IBM as a CPU vendor (+40 pp), which is one of the three vendors in the shop; installation through terminal commands (+36 pp), the only installation method shown in WordRush; the ARM architecture (+28 pp), which the shop recommends for lower energy consumption; DDoS (+28 pp); and website hosting (+28 pp). \section{Discussion and Limitations}

Regarding RQ1, recognition increased for most terms embedded in the game's mechanics. Regarding RQ2, options absent from the game, such as ransomware, did not show a comparable increase, which is consistent with an effect of exposure to the game content rather than a generic improvement. Regarding RQ3, the most severe problems concerned feature discoverability (opening the shop), feedback (e-mails) and the cannon controls.

These results must be interpreted with caution. The study used a small convenience sample, a single short session (about 19 minutes) and an immediate post-test, with no comparison group and no retention test. A single-group pre/post design cannot separate the effect of playing from that of repeating the same questionnaire, and a few unchanged items do not establish causation. Participants received help when blocked for more than two minutes, the sessions were remote, and the analysis is descriptive, as the questionnaires could not be paired. The instrument measures recognition: selecting ``terminal commands'' does not show that a participant can install software or explain a command, and recognising DDoS does not show knowledge of how to mitigate it; management decisions were not assessed. Finally, one item used incorrect terminology (Apache), and the technical content would benefit from review by data centre professionals.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented the design, development and exploratory evaluation of \textbf{Port 0}, a resource management simulation game designed to expose players to data centre concepts through server configuration, a command-typing minigame and a periodic attack loop. In a study with 25 participants, recognition increased for most terms embedded in the gameplay, such as the ARM architecture, IBM as a CPU vendor, terminal-based installation and DDoS attacks, whereas content not addressed by the game showed no comparable change. Usability observations and telemetry identified interface problems that guided subsequent improvements.

Given the limitations of the study, these findings are preliminary indications that mechanics-driven simulation games can introduce technical vocabulary, not evidence that players acquire data centre management skills. Future work will expand the operational challenges of Port 0, introduce further security scenarios such as ransomware, include items that assess conceptual understanding and management decisions, re-evaluate the usability changes and conduct controlled studies with larger samples and delayed post-tests to assess retention.
